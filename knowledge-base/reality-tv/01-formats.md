# 1. Reality formats, taken apart

Reality TV is a family of **social experiments with a camera and an
edit**. Under the surface variety the working formats reuse a small set of
mechanics. Each mechanic below is listed with *what it does to the agents*
and *what it does to the audience* - because a mechanic only earns its place
if it does both.

## 1.1 The formats, by their core mechanic

| Format (first run) | Core mechanic | Pressure on agents | What the audience gets |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| *An American Family* (1973), *The Real World* (1992) | strangers living together, filmed | propinquity, friction over shared space | intimacy, slow-burn conflict |
| *Expedition Robinson* / *Survivor* (1997/2000) | tribes, challenges, **peer elimination**, a **jury of the eliminated** picks the winner | must form coalitions yet not burn the people who will judge you | strategy, betrayal, the "blindside" |
| *Big Brother* (1999) | **confinement**, total surveillance, **diary room**, nominations, public or housemate eviction | isolation from outside info, boredom, scarcity of privacy | voyeurism, the confessional's inner view, voting power |
| *The Mole* (1999) | a **hidden saboteur** among the team; quiz on who it is | suspicion, reading others | a whodunit across a season |
| *Temptation Island* (2001) | couples separated, surrounded by attractive singles | commitment against high-quality alternatives | will they cheat? |
| *The Bachelor* (2002) | one chooser, many suitors, **rose ceremony** | competition for one resource, jealousy | elimination ritual, romance fantasy |
| *Paradise Hotel* (2003), *Love Island* (2005/2015) | **must be coupled or leave**; **recoupling** ceremonies; **bombshell** arrivals; public vote | coupling as survival and as love - the two always confused | love vs strategy ambiguity, triangles on schedule |
| *Golden Balls* (2007) | final **Split or Steal** (a one-shot prisoner's dilemma with talk) | trust under money | the moment of the reveal |
| *Jersey Shore* (2009) | archetype-cast house, alcohol, nightlife | low inhibition, tribal loyalty | slapstick, feuds, catchphrases |
| *Are You the One?* (2014) | hidden correct pairings; **truth booth** and **beam ceremony** reveal partial information; shared prize | collective inference vs personal desire | a puzzle the audience solves alongside |
| *Terrace House* (2012) | low stakes, slice of life, commentary panel | ordinary courtship | calm, warmth, commentary as a second show |
| *The Genius* (2013, Korea) | social-deduction mini-games, alliances, elimination match | strategic betrayal at high IQ | elegant schemes, double-crosses |
| *The Circle* (2018) | **anonymity** - players meet only through text on screens; ratings; catfishing allowed | presentation vs truth; trust without bodies | dramatic irony (we know who is real) |
| *Too Hot to Handle* (2019) | **rules against touching**, breaches cost a **shared prize pool** | individual temptation vs collective cost (a commons) | public goods game made sexy |
| *Love Is Blind* (2020) | dating through a wall (**voice only**), engagement before sight | attachment formed apart from looks | the "reveal", then reality testing |
| *The Traitors* (2021) | **hidden roles** (traitors "murder" at night; faithful banish at a **round table**); shared prize | deception, paranoia, scapegoating | perfect dramatic irony: we know who the traitors are |
| *Physical 100*, *Squid Game: The Challenge* (2023) | mass elimination, games with moral dilemmas | survival, kindness vs advantage | spectacle and moral choice |

Two structural families:

- **Social-survival games** (Survivor, Big Brother, Traitors, Genius): a
  prize, elimination by peers or public, alliances, hidden information.
  Emergence is strategic.
- **Romance houses** (Love Island, Bachelor, Temptation Island, Too Hot to
  Handle, Love Is Blind): coupling as the scored act, temptation, arrivals.
  Emergence is affective.

The strongest shows **cross the two**: in Love Island coupling is both love
and survival, so every act is ambiguous - and ambiguity is what the
audience argues about. That cross is the design target (*love or money*).

## 1.2 The mechanics, one by one

**Confinement and isolation.** No phones, no news, no outside friends. In
psychology, isolation raises dependence on the in-group and the value of
every in-house relationship (Schachter 1959: anxiety raises affiliation).
It shrinks a contestant's world to the cast, so every small social signal
weighs more. *Model:* the house is the agent's entire evidence source; prior
relationships are distant memories that decay.

**Propinquity and forced proximity.** Who sleeps near whom predicts who
bonds (Festinger, Schachter & Back 1950: friendships in a dorm followed
doorway distance). Bed allocation, shared tasks and room assignment are
real levers. *Model:* encounter rate between two agents by layout; liking
grows with mere exposure.

**Scarcity.** Of beds, privacy, food, partners (an odd number of singles),
and safety (someone goes each week). Scarcity makes every relation zero-sum
somewhere. *Model:* a resource that cannot be shared by all, always.

**Elimination - who decides.** By peers (Survivor), by the public (BB UK,
Love Island), by a chooser (Bachelor), by a game (Squid Game). Each makes a
different mind: peer elimination makes politicians; public elimination
makes performers (they play to the camera they cannot see); chooser
elimination makes rivals. *Model:* agents' theory of who decides shapes
their strategy - an agent that believes the public decides will optimise a
*public image* belief.

**The jury of the eliminated.** Survivor's masterstroke: the people you
betray decide whether you win. It forces strategies to trade off now against
later and makes betrayal costly in a delayed, uncertain way. *Model:* a
reputation account held by the eliminated, still updating from what they
hear.

**Hidden roles and hidden information.** The Mole, Traitors, Werewolf/Mafia
(Davidoff 1986). Paranoia, false accusation and scapegoating are the
product. The audience is told the truth, so every scene is dramatic irony.
*Model:* a role variable with a prior over who holds it; agents run
inference; traitors run deception policies.

**Partial-information reveals.** Are You the One's truth booth (one pair:
match or not) and beam count (how many pairs are right) turn the house into
a collective Bayesian inference problem whose answers arrive on a
schedule. *Model:* reveals as evidence events with exact likelihoods.

**Recoupling / ceremony.** A periodic, public, fixed-form event where
private states are forced into public commitments: rose, recoupling,
tribal council, round table, nomination. Rituals synchronise the cast and
the audience, convert private uncertainty into public fact, and give the
week its rhythm (build-up -> peak -> fallout). *Model:* a ritual is a state
of its own with roles and a fixed sequence - see [04-models.md](04-models.md)
*Rituals*.

**Arrivals (bombshells) and twists.** An exogenous shock - a new attractive
single, a returning ex, a rule change - raises the quality of alternatives
in every relationship at once. Investment-model theory (Rusbult 1980) says
commitment drops exactly when alternatives improve. *Model:* an arrival is
a new agent entering at a seam, chosen (by the producer or the audience) for
maximum expected conflict.

**Temptation with a shared cost.** Too Hot to Handle makes kissing a
public-goods defection: private benefit, collective cost, public blame. A
commons with gossip. *Model:* breach events debit a shared pool and are
broadcast; norm enforcement emerges from reputation.

**The confessional / diary room.** The contestant's private view, spoken
to camera. It gives the audience *access to minds* - the thing that makes
dramatic irony possible. Goffman's front stage and back stage (1959) made a
format. *Model:* free in a simulation: the audience can be shown the mind
state directly (beliefs as overlays, "thought bubbles"), or a confessional
can be rendered from the mind's current top concerns as templated lines.

**Anonymity and the screen.** The Circle and Love Is Blind separate
presentation from body. Trust is built on text; catfishing is legal. Note
the parallel to the simulation: a mind receives *content*, never pixels.

**The edit and story producers.** Real shows find stories after the fact:
loggers tag footage, story producers assemble arcs (the hero, the villain
edit, the redemption), sometimes stitching sentences from separate clips
("frankenbiting"). *Model:* a story sifter (Ryan 2018; Kreminski) that
watches the event log and picks threads; the edit chooses which camera and
which thread - honestly, from what happened.

## 1.3 Casting: archetypes

Producers cast for collision, not for likeability. Recurring roles:

| Archetype | Function in the house |
| --- | --- |
| the sweetheart | a loved character whose pain the audience feels (disposition: we want them rewarded) |
| the villain | moral clarity; the audience wants them punished; they drive plots |
| the strategist / puppet master | schemes the audience can follow - dramatic irony |
| the flirt / player | moves through couples; generates triangles |
| the loyal partner | a stake to betray |
| the wildcard | high impulsivity, low predictability: surprise source |
| the mother hen / peacemaker | mediates; restores balance; resets feuds |
| the underdog | starts disliked or unlucky; arc of rising |
| the clown | relief between peaks (pacing) |
| the outsider | low belonging; recruitment target for coalitions |

Archetypes are *regions of trait space* (see [03-priors.md](03-priors.md)),
not scripts: a villain is low agreeableness + low honesty-humility + high
extraversion; a wildcard high neuroticism + low conscientiousness + high
openness. Casting = sampling the population prior, then choosing a set that
maximises expected conflict and variety (a determinantal or
diversity-maximising pick).

## 1.4 The producers' tricks (and their honest analogues)

Real productions are documented to use sleep restriction, alcohol, no
reading material, long waits before ceremonies, interviews timed after
upsets, and rules that force pairs together. These lower inhibition, raise
arousal, raise dependence on the group, and keep emotions near the surface.

In a simulation they become **world parameters**, not hidden cheats:
fatigue and arousal are variables in each mind; alcohol a state that
lowers inhibition and raises impulsivity; boredom a drive that raises
novelty-seeking (Pask's bored machine, [05-cybernetics.md](05-cybernetics.md)).
Misattribution of arousal (Dutton & Aron 1974, the shaky bridge) is a real
effect worth using: a frightening task raises attraction to whoever is
beside you.

## 1.5 Why audiences watch (what the studies say)

- **Status and vengeance.** Reiss & Wiltz (2004): the basic desire that most
  predicted enjoying reality TV was *status* (feeling more important than
  the people watched), then vengeance. Viewers enjoy judging.
- **Social comparison and gossip.** Reality TV is gossip about people one
  "knows" (parasocial ties, Horton & Wohl 1956). Gossip is how humans learn
  norms (Dunbar 1996; Baumeister et al 2004).
- **Voyeurism and authenticity.** Nabi et al (2003): enjoyment linked to
  seeing "real" people unguarded; the *authentic moment* (a crack in the
  performance) is the prize.
- **Moral judgement.** Affective disposition theory (Zillmann): we enjoy
  liked characters rewarded and disliked ones punished, and suspense is
  strongest when we care about the outcome.
- **Prediction.** Viewers form theories and bet (formally or not) - hidden
  information formats are puzzles.

## 1.6 What reality TV gets wrong, and what a simulation can do better

| Weakness of real shows | Simulation's answer |
| --- | --- |
| harm to real people (aftercare failures; suicides of former contestants, e.g. Terrace House 2020) | the cast is simulated; harm stays fiction |
| the edit fabricates (frankenbiting) and the audience knows it | every shown moment is true to the world's causes; the audience can audit |
| only the producers see everything | the audience can switch point of view, open minds, follow a thread |
| one season, then the cast leaves | persistent alumni with real memories; old grudges return; dynasties |
| casting for one dimension (looks) | traits as a full distribution; looks independent from personality (Love Is Blind's insight) |
| fake twists that feel arbitrary | twists drawn from the audience or the producer's suspense model, and visibly caused |
| the audience only votes once a week | continuous, world-mediated interventions with costs (see [07-show.md](07-show.md)) |
| boredom between peaks | pacing director keeps tension in a cycle; low-tension scenes become comedy and warmth |
| same formula every season | the format itself is data: mechanics on and off, rules changing by vote |

Closest prior art in games: **Tomodachi Life** (Nintendo, 2013) - cute Mii
characters in an apartment block fall in love, fight, confess, get
rejected; much loved, mostly scripted relations. **The Sims** (needs,
autonomy), **Animal Crossing** (personality types), **Prom Week** (social
physics), **Versu** (social practices), **Talk of the Town** (knowledge
that travels and mutates), **RimWorld** (a storyteller that paces events),
**Crusader Kings** (traits and opinions making dynastic drama), **Dwarf
Fortress** (emergent history). See [04-models.md](04-models.md).
