# 5. Cybernetics, including what was buried

Cybernetics (Wiener 1948: "control and communication in the animal and the
machine") is the study of systems that steer by feedback. It was eclipsed
in the 1970s by symbolic AI and by its own political fortunes; several of
its best ideas were dropped, banned or forgotten. Many are exactly what a
cheap, emergent, entertaining social simulation needs. Each section ends
with **use:** - what it becomes in the show.

## 5.1 First-order cybernetics

**Negative feedback and homeostasis** (Wiener; Rosenblueth, Wiener &
Bigelow 1943, "Behavior, Purpose and Teleology"): purpose as error
correction toward a goal. *Use:* every need and every relationship target
is a set point; behaviour is what reduces the error.

**Ashby's homeostat and ultrastability** (*Design for a Brain*, 1952).
Four coupled units; when an **essential variable** leaves its bounds, a
unit *randomly rewires its own parameters* (a step function) until the
whole settles again. Adaptation without a plan: try new behaviour at
random when the old keeps you out of bounds. *Use:* the **meltdown and
reinvention** mechanic. When a character's essential variables (belonging,
safety, self-esteem) stay out of bounds too long, they jump: re-draw a few
policy weights (go cold, turn schemer, quit, declare love). Random but
caused, and legible: "she snapped". Also the RimWorld mental break, with a
theory behind it.

**Law of requisite variety** (Ashby 1956): only variety can absorb
variety - a regulator must have at least as many responses as the
disturbances it must counter. *Use:* (1) the producer cannot steer a house
with fewer levers than the house has ways to go flat; (2) a character with
few responses is predictable and dull; the cast as a whole must have more
behavioural variety than the audience can predict.

**Good regulator theorem** (Conant & Ashby 1970): every good regulator of
a system must be a model of it. *Use:* the producer and each strategic
agent need a model of others - theory of mind is required, not optional.

## 5.2 Second-order cybernetics

**Von Foerster's non-trivial machines** (1970s): a trivial machine maps
input to output fixed; a non-trivial one has internal state that changes
with each input, so its behaviour depends on its history - deterministic
but unpredictable from outside. *Use:* characters must be non-trivial
machines to the audience: same provocation, different reaction, because of
what happened Tuesday. Memory and mood make this so.

**Eigenforms** (von Foerster; Kauffman): stable objects as fixed points of
recursive observation - what persists when a process is applied to its own
output. *Use:* reputations and nicknames. Each retelling of "she's the
fake one" is shaped by the last; the label that survives recursive gossip
is an eigenform. Running gossip to its fixed point predicts the house's
"official story" of each person.

**Observing systems / the observer included**: the audience and the
producer are inside the loop. *Use:* when characters learn they are
watched (Big Brother format), performing for the audience is a goal;
public-vote formats create agents that model the audience.

**Bateson: schismogenesis** (*Naven*, 1936). Two kinds of escalating
interaction: **symmetrical** (boast meets boast, insult meets insult -
rivalry escalates) and **complementary** (dominance invites submission
invites more dominance; nagging and withdrawal). Without restraint both
run away to a split. *Use:* feuds and toxic couples, from two rules:
`hostility_ij += k * hostility_ji` (symmetrical) and `dom_ij += k * sub_ji`
(complementary), with a saturating restraint (fatigue, a peacemaker, an
apology, a ritual). The anxious-avoidant pursuer-distancer couple is
complementary schismogenesis.

**Bateson: double bind** (1956): contradictory demands at two levels
("be spontaneous!"; "stay coupled, but follow your heart") with no exit and
no way to comment on the contradiction. *Use:* the format's rules can
deliberately create double binds (love vs the prize; loyalty vs the
coalition) - stress with nowhere to go makes drama, and the confessional is
where it is spoken.

**Bateson: learning levels** (deutero-learning): learning to learn - an
agent learns not only what B does but what kind of game is being played.
*Use:* contestants who notice the format ("bombshells always arrive after
a stable week") and pre-empt it. The producer must vary its pattern.

**Maruyama: the second cybernetics** (1963): *deviation-amplifying* mutual
causal processes - positive feedback creates structure (a city grows where
a farmer settled by chance). *Use:* small random advantages amplify into
house hierarchies, cliques and the "power couple"; a slight early on
becomes a season-long feud. The cure for flat runs is amplifying loops,
the cure for chaos is damping ones - design both, explicitly.

## 5.3 Management cybernetics: Stafford Beer

**The Viable System Model** (*Brain of the Firm*, 1972): any viable system
has five subsystems - operations (1), coordination (2), control (3),
audit (3*), intelligence about the outside (4) and identity/policy (5) -
recursively. *Use:* a structure for the **producer**: S1 the house; S2 the
schedules and rituals that keep it from oscillating; S3 the weekly drama
budget; S3* the story sifter auditing what really happened; S4 the
audience model (what will they want next); S5 the show's identity ("this
is a show about love and money").

**Algedonic signals**: pain/pleasure signals that bypass the hierarchy
and go straight to the top when something matters. **Project Cybersyn**
(Chile, 1971-73) wired factories to an operations room; it ended with the
coup. Its least-known part, **Cyberfolk**, proposed **algedonic meters in
homes**: a dial each citizen turns from unhappy to happy, aggregated in
real time and fed to government. That is continuous audience voting, half
a century early. *Use:* the audience's channel. A viewer turns a dial
(per character, per scene, per couple); the sum is the algedonic signal;
it reaches the world only through the producer's levers or as in-world
events, never a write into a mind.

## 5.4 Pask: the machine that gets bored

**Musicolour** (Pask, 1953): a light show driven by a band's sound, which
*habituated*: if the music repeated, the machine's response faded and it
moved its attention elsewhere; musicians had to keep varying to keep it
lit. A performer and a machine in **conversation**, each adapting to the
other. **Conversation Theory** (1975): understanding as agreement reached
by exchange between two learners. *Use:* (1) the audience model habituates
exactly so (02, *repetition*); (2) characters habituate to each other's
tricks - the third time the flirt flirts it stops working; (3) a model for
the audience-show relation: the show must keep changing to keep the
audience lit, the audience changes what lights it.

## 5.5 Perceptual Control Theory (Powers 1973)

Behaviour is the control of perception: an organism acts so that what it
perceives matches an internal reference, in a hierarchy where higher
levels set the references of lower ones (intensity, sensation,
configuration, transition, event, relationship, category, sequence,
program, principle, system concept). Each loop is trivial:
`error = reference - perception; output += gain * error * dt`. Long
marginalised by mainstream psychology; experimentally strong on tracking
tasks. *Use:*
- A cheap, stable controller per goal, linear (fast, checkable).
- **Conflict as two controllers on one variable**: two people who want
  different values of the same perception (how close A sits to B; who
  leads the group) push against each other; outputs escalate until one
  gives up or they reach a stalemate - drama, with a mechanism.
- **Reorganisation** (Powers): persistent error triggers random change of
  the controller's parameters - Ashby's ultrastability again.

## 5.6 Soviet cybernetics and reflexive control

Cybernetics was condemned in Stalin's USSR as "bourgeois pseudoscience"
(1950s), rehabilitated after 1955, then flourished (Glushkov's OGAS, a
planned national computer network, never built). From it:

**Lefebvre's reflexive control** (1960s-80s): influencing an adversary by
transmitting to them the grounds from which they will *themselves* derive
the decision you want - shaping their picture of you, of the situation,
and of what you think they think. Reflexive *ranks*: I think, I think you
think, I think you think I think... The theory was adopted in Soviet
military doctrine and is the intellectual root of modern information
operations. *Use:* the deep strategist's toolkit - plant evidence, stage
a scene for a watcher, let a rumour reach someone "by accident" so they
trust it more (overheard beats told). Agents with second-order beliefs can
do it with bounded rank 2-3.

**Lefebvre's algebra of conscience** (1982, 2001): a formal model of a
subject choosing between a positive and a negative pole, in which the
choice depends on the world's pressure, the subject's past experience, and
the subject's intention, with the subject's image of itself included. It
predicts a self-evaluation bias near 0.62 (the golden section), with some
experimental support **(verify formula and data in Lefebvre 2001)**. Two
"ethical systems" (one where compromise between good and evil is evil,
one where it is good) give opposite characters. *Use:* a cheap formula for
moral choice under pressure, with built-in self-image; and two moral types
of contestant that misunderstand each other.

## 5.7 Sociometry, field theory, and affect control

**Moreno's sociometry** (*Who Shall Survive?*, 1934): the **sociogram** -
who chooses whom for what (to sit near, to work with, to room with) - and
*tele*, the mutual flow of attraction. Moreno reassigned girls at the
Hudson school by sociometric choice and cut runaways. He also invented
**psychodrama**: people act out their conflicts in front of a group, with
a director. Reality TV is psychodrama with a camera. *Use:* the house's
relational state *is* a set of signed, weighted sociograms (affection,
trust, rivalry, desire); sociometric tests (who would you keep?) are
rituals that make them public.

**Lewin's field theory**: `B = f(P, E)` - behaviour is a function of the
person and their environment, as forces in a life space. Approach and
avoidance gradients (Miller 1944): avoidance rises more steeply than
approach as a goal nears - the classic approach-avoidance conflict that
makes someone walk toward the person they like and stop short. *Use:* the
fear and hope vectors as forces with different gradients in distance and
time: hope dominates far off, fear close up (cold feet before a
confession).

**Affect Control Theory** (Heise 1979, 2007; Smith-Lovin). Every identity
("flirt", "friend", "traitor"), behaviour ("compliment", "betray") and
setting has a measured **EPA** sentiment - Evaluation (good-bad), Potency
(strong-weak), Activity (lively-quiet) - in published dictionaries of
thousands of words, by culture. An event "actor behaves toward object"
creates transient impressions by empirically fitted equations; people act
to **minimise deflection** between transient impressions and fundamental
sentiments, and re-label people (identity change) when deflection stays
high. Mainstream psychology mostly ignores it; it is quantitative,
calibrated, and cheap (small linear-quadratic equations). BayesACT adds
uncertainty. *Use:* the **verbal layer without an LLM**: event templates
come with EPA values; an agent chooses the behaviour that keeps the scene
coherent with who it thinks it and the other are; when a "friend" keeps
doing "betray", the deflection forces a relabelling - "you're not my friend
any more" is a computed identity change. The labels are the words the
audience reads.

## 5.8 System dynamics (Forrester; Meadows)

Stocks, flows and feedback delays (Forrester's *Industrial Dynamics*,
1961; *Urban Dynamics*, 1969: policies produce counterintuitive results;
*Limits to Growth*, 1972, attacked and later found to track history
fairly well). Meadows' *leverage points* (1999): parameters are weak
levers; feedback loops stronger; information flows stronger; rules
stronger still; goals and paradigms strongest. *Use:* the producer and the
audience have levers of different strength - changing a prize amount
(parameter) does little; revealing a secret (information flow) or changing
a rule (no more coupling required) reshapes the house. Price audience
powers by leverage.

## 5.9 Other forgotten or side-lined threads worth taking

- **Kauffman's random Boolean networks** (1969): networks at the edge of
  chaos (connectivity ~2) are both stable and adaptable. *Use:* tune the
  house's coupling - too few ties, nothing propagates; too many, chaos.
  Aim for criticality: perturbations spread sometimes, not always.
- **Luhmann: double contingency** (1984): each party's action depends on
  what it expects the other expects; social systems are made of
  communications, not people. *Use:* gossip is the house's own system;
  the story is in the communications.
- **Goffman's frame analysis and face-work** (1967, 1974): interactions
  protect face; embarrassment is a frame break. *Use:* face as a variable;
  public humiliation is a face loss with a known cost.
- **Girard's mimetic theory** (1961, 1972): desire imitates; rivalry
  spreads; communities resolve it by a **scapegoat** - unanimous violence
  against one, which restores peace. *Use:* elimination rituals are
  scapegoat mechanisms; expect the house to converge on one target when
  tension is high, and the target to be the one least embedded.
- **Interaction ritual chains** (Collins 2004) - see 04 *Rituals*.
- **Rapoport's tournaments** (Axelrod 1984, from Rapoport's tit-for-tat) -
  reciprocity as an evolutionarily stable habit.

## 5.10 What cybernetics adds, in summary

| Need of the show | Cybernetic answer |
| --- | --- |
| characters that stay themselves yet surprise | non-trivial machines: fixed traits, history-dependent state |
| meltdowns and reinventions | ultrastability / PCT reorganisation |
| feuds and toxic couples | schismogenesis, two controllers on one variable |
| triangles and scapegoats | mimetic desire, balance |
| reputations | eigenforms of recursive gossip |
| strategy and manipulation | reflexive control, rank-bounded |
| the verbal layer, no LLM | affect control theory: EPA words, deflection |
| an audience that shapes the world | algedonic signals (Cyberfolk) |
| an audience that must not get bored | Musicolour habituation |
| a producer that steers without cheating | VSM; requisite variety; leverage points |
